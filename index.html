I

The Meeting of Two Worlds

At the western gate of the Mediterranean, Africa reaches toward Europe across a sliver of sea. Here, where the Atlantic breathes into an ancient basin, two continents nearly touch — separated by just 14 kilometres of churning water. This narrow passage has shaped civilisations, migrations, and ecosystems for millions of years.

II

The Geographic Funnel

The Strait of Gibraltar is nature's most dramatic bottleneck. At its narrowest — between Punta de Tarifa and Punta Cires — the channel squeezes to barely 10–11 km. Every marine creature migrating between the Atlantic and Mediterranean must pass through this gauntlet. There is no alternative route.

🐋 Obligatory migration corridor
⚓ 58 km total length

At its narrowest, the strait is roughly the distance of a half-marathon. On a clear day, you can see Africa from the coast of Spain.

III

The Hidden Landscape Below

Beneath the surface lies a dramatic terrain. The Camarinal Sill — a massive submarine ridge — rises from ~900 m to just 280 m below the waves, aligned with Punta Camarinal on the Spanish coast. This underwater barrier forces deep currents upward, creating turbulence, nutrient upwelling, and the conditions that make this strait one of the most biologically productive waters on Earth.

IV

The Ocean Engine

The Strait operates as a colossal two-layer pump. Cool, nutrient-rich Atlantic water flows east along the surface, while warmer, saltier Mediterranean outflow slides westward along the bottom. This thermohaline exchange creates powerful currents that dictate where marine life concentrates — and where predators learn to hunt.

➡️ Surface: Atlantic → Mediterranean
⬅️ Deep: Mediterranean → Atlantic

V

The Tuna Route & the Orcas

Every spring, Atlantic bluefin tuna (Thunnus thynnus) surge through the Strait on their ancestral spawning migration. And the orcas know. A resident community of 39 individuals (as of 2011) has learned to intercept the tuna here, developing sophisticated hunting techniques — including "espartes", cooperative drives that exploit the narrow geography.

VI

The Almadraba

For over 3,000 years, humans have set great labyrinth-nets called almadrabas along these coasts — trapping tuna as they pass. This ancient practice, inherited from Phoenician and Roman fishers, has coexisted with orcas across millennia. In Tarifa alone (2004), 48 boats landed 597 tons, while orcas depredated just 42 tuna from the fleet. A fragile equilibrium, sustained by tradition and respect for the sea.
