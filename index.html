The 14K Gateway

The Strait of Gibraltar has shaped civilisations, migrations, and ecosystems for millions of years. Around 5.96 million years ago, the modern Strait did not exist. The collision of the African and Eurasian plates closed the Strait, isolating the Mediterranean Sea and leading to its near-total desiccation — the Messinian Salinity Crisis. Basin sea levels dropped by ~1,500 m, depositing a massive, kilometer-thick 'salt giant' (>1M km³ of evaporites) across the abyssal plains. Later, 5.33 million years ago, an Atlantic megafloood refilled the Mediterranean Sea through a process known as the Zanclean flood. The two basins were reconnected and the Strait of Gibraltar was established as the only gateway for marine life to pass between the Atlantic and Mediterranean.

The Meeting of Two Worlds

Beneath the surface, lies the Strait's grandest barrier: the Camarinal Sill. It is the shallowest underwater mountain connecting Europe and Africa (at a water depth of ~280 m), breaking up the surrounding abysses that reach depths of up to 1,000 m. In the upper 150 m, cool,low-salinity Atlantic water flows eastward. Beneath this surface inflow the dense Mediterranean outflows westward. This is an area of intense currents since when Atlantic and Mediterranean water masses collide nutrient-rich waters upwell along the seafloor. This makes the sector extending from Punta Camarinal to "Las Bajas" a historical fishing ground.

Bluefin Tuna Migration

Swimming from the Gulf of Mexico to the Mediterranean Sea and back again, the Atlantic bluefin tuna (Thunnus thynnus) surges in spring to spawn in the Med and returns in summer to feed in the Atlantic. Riding the shallow surface Atlantic inflow eastward and returning through the cold, deep Mediterranean outflow, the tuna dictate the cadence of the Strait. Local fishermen adapt their artisanal techniques to harvest them—from coastal trap-nets when they enter the Strait to deep drop-lines when they exit. Interestingly, resident Iberian killer whales adapt alongside humans fishing techniques.

Fishermen

For over 3,000 years, humans have set great labyrinth-nets called almadrabas along the Spanish Atlantic coasts — trapping tuna as they pass. This ancient practice, inherited from Phoenician and Roman fishers, has coexisted with orcas across millennia. This technique is used when they pass through the strait. In Tarifa alone (2004), 48 boats landed 597 tons, while orcas depredated just 42 tuna from the fleet.
