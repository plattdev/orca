The 14K Gateway

The Strait of Gibraltar has shaped civilisations, migrations, and ecosystems for millions of years. Around 5.96 million years ago, the modern Strait did not exist. The collision of the African and Eurasian plates closed the Strait, isolating the Mediterranean Sea and leading to its near-total desiccation — the Messinian Salinity Crisis. Basin sea levels dropped by ~1,500 m, depositing a massive, kilometer-thick 'salt giant' (>1M km³ of evaporites) across the abyssal plains. Later, 5.33 million years ago, an Atlantic megaflood refilled the Mediterranean Sea through a process known as the Zanclean flood. The two basins were reconnected and the Strait of Gibraltar was established as the only gateway for marine life to pass between the Atlantic and Mediterranean.

The Meeting of Two Worlds

Beneath the surface, lies the Strait's grandest barrier: the Camarinal Sill. It is the shallowest underwater mountain connecting Europe and Africa (at a water depth of ~280 m), breaking up the surrounding abysses that reach depths of up to 1,000 m. In the upper 150 m, cool, low-salinity Atlantic water flows eastward. Beneath this surface inflow the dense Mediterranean outflows westward. This is an area of intense currents since when Atlantic and Mediterranean water masses collide nutrient-rich waters upwell along the seafloor. This makes the sector extending from Punta Camarinal to "Las Bajas" a historical fishing ground.

Bluefin Tuna Migration

Swimming from the Gulf of Mexico to the Mediterranean Sea and back again, the Atlantic bluefin tuna (Thunnus thynnus) surges in spring to spawn in the Med and returns in summer to feed in the Atlantic. Electronic tagging studies by Block et al. (2005) and Rooker et al. (2008) tracked hundreds of fish across the Atlantic basin, revealing two populations — western spawners homing to the Gulf of Mexico and eastern spawners returning to the Mediterranean — that overlap on open-ocean foraging grounds. Migrating adults passing through the Strait average 187 kg (ranging from 170 cm to 290 cm in length). Riding the shallow surface Atlantic inflow eastward and returning through the cold, deep Mediterranean outflow at depths between 290 m and 500 m, the tuna dictate the cadence of the Strait. Local fishermen adapt their artisanal techniques to harvest them — from coastal trap-nets when they enter the Strait to deep drop-lines when they exit. Interestingly, resident Iberian killer whales adapt alongside human fishing techniques.

The Iberian Orcas

The entire resident community of Iberian killer whales consists of just 39 individuals, structured into 5 matrilineal pods. When actively hunting free-swimming tuna, orcas engage in prolonged pursuits lasting up to 30 minutes at sustained speeds of 3.7 to 6.0 m/s (~13–21 km/h). However, active endurance-exhaustion hunting can only catch small-to-medium tuna (0.8 m to 1.5 m long, weighing 20–70 kg); larger tuna exceed the orcas' aerobic limits for open-water chases. iller whale reproduction in the Strait is extremely sensitive to prey availability. When bluefin tuna stocks dropped, lactating mothers could not secure sufficient calories through depredation, resulting in nutritional stress (and potentially mobilizing bioaccumulated toxic pollutants stored in blubber), which caused total calf mortality across the interacting pods.

Fishermen & 'Almadraba'

In spring, for over 3,000 years, humans have set great labyrinth-nets called almadrabas along the Spanish Atlantic coasts — trapping tuna as they pass west-to-east on their journey to spawn. Riding the shallow Atlantic inflow, the tuna face a dual trial: human nets anchored along the shoreline and pods of Iberian orcas hunting in the open shallows, driving the fish to exhaustion in rapid, sustained pursuits.

Co-Hunting

In summer, when tuna return to the Atlantic, a different artisanal technique takes over: 'drop-line' fishery (pesca de la piedra). Again, orcas-tuna-fisheremen converge on an underwater plateau called 'Las Bajas' — a 2 nautical mile × 1 nautical mile shelf (6.9 km²) located midway between Tarifa and Tangier. Every 6-hour tidal cycle, rising waters create turbulence over the seafloor that upwells nutrients and concentrates fish, turning Las Bajas into the arena where tuna, orcas, and fishermen collide. Here, small 5–6 meter Moroccan wooden boats and Spanish artisanal vessels deploy a 10–14 kg stone tied with a false knot to lower three baited hooks down to 300+ meters. When the stone hits bottom, fishermen pull the line to snap the knot, leaving the stone on the seabed as the baited hooks float upward. Hauling a hooked tuna back up by hand takes ~20 minutes. On a typical summer day, 50–60 Moroccan boats and 10–20 Spanish vessels gather at Las Bajas, creating a density of about 8.7 fishing lines per km². Each boat drops about 18 stones per 6-hour tide, hooking 1 tuna for every 10 stone drops.Within the Iberian Orca subpopulation, 2 out of 5 pods have learned to steal large tuna hooked in artisanal drop-line fisheries. Rather than diving 300+ meters, the orcas wait in the top 2 to 50 meters. Video analysis reveals that orcas swim along the vertical lines, pressing their pectoral fins, snouts, melons, and bellies against the ropes to feel line tension and weight vibrations — detecting whether a fish is hooked below. This cultural strategy spread in less than a generation after stone fishing was introduced in 1995.

Three Titans. One Strait.
A Story Unfinished.

The Strait of Gibraltar remains one of the last places on Earth where apex predators, ancient fisheries, and marine biodiversity intersect in a living, breathing equilibrium — one that demands our understanding and protection. A fragile equilibrium, sustained by tradition and respect for the sea.
A romantic hypothesis has arisen among some researchers based on the fact that Neanderthals may have hunted in cooperation with orcas — killer whales driving exhausted tuna toward shallow shores, and humans scavenging or assisting at the beach. Given that Neanderthals were last thought to inhabit the coastal caves of Gibraltar, could feeding on tuna facilitated by orcas have prolonged their survival in this location? Let's hope that the geological and archaeological records help one day answer this question.

Such cross-species cooperation is far from unprecedented. In Twofold Bay (Eden, Australia), shore-based whalers — both Indigenous Thawa Aboriginals and European crews — cooperated for nearly a century with wild orca pods to hunt migrating baleen whales, honoring an unwritten pact known as the 'Law of the Tongue' where the killer whales received the tongue and lips before humans towed the carcass. Modeling this historical mutualism through fuzzy rule-based logic, Coppola et al. (2015) demonstrated that mutual trust and reciprocal cooperation, rather than simple opportunistic encounters, were the decisive factors that sustained this rare human-cetacean alliance over generations until outside disruption severed the bond.
